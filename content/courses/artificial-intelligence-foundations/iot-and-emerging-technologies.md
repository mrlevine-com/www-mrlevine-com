## Lesson Overview
### How do IoT systems transform everyday objects into “smart” devices, and what challenges arise with their use?
{{% define "Internet of Things (IoT)" %}}

Watch on YouTube: [What is IoT?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6mBO2vqLv38)

A standalone device runs on its own and doesn't share its data with anything else. An IoT device takes that same idea and connects it to the internet, which unlocks new features a standalone device can't offer, like remote access, real-time alerts, and automatic updates:

- A basic pedometer counts your steps and stores them on the device. A fitness tracker counts your steps too, but also tracks things like your heart rate and location, then sends that data to an app so you can see your health trends over time.
- A digital camera stores photos and videos on a memory card. A Ring camera captures the same kind of footage, but streams it to your phone over Wi-Fi so you can check your front door from anywhere.
- A home stereo system just plays audio from a connected source. A smart speaker plays audio too, but also listens for your voice commands and sends them to the cloud to be processed.

That same connectivity that gives IoT devices their new features also introduces new risks. Once data leaves a device, every step of its journey (from the device, across a network, to a company's servers) is a chance for it to be exposed in a data breach, intercepted by someone else, or used in a way you didn't expect or agree to.

### How does data travel between devices?
Once an IoT device collects data, it has to transmit that data somewhere else to be processed. The right transmission method depends on how far the data needs to travel:

| Transmission | Range | Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Near Field Communication (NFC) | About 1 inch | Secure, close-range interactions | Credit cards, Tap to Pay, mobile payments |
| Bluetooth | About 30 feet | Wireless connections within a single room or building | Headphones, smartwatches, fitness trackers |
| Wi-Fi | 100–300 feet | Reliable connections within a building or campus | Home routers, laptops, smart speakers |
| Cellular (5G) | 0.5–1 mile | Mobile connections across cities or rural areas | Smartphones, connected cars |
| Satellite | 50+ miles | Global connections from space | The Starlink Network, GPS |

### Compare a Standalone Device to an IoT Device
Choose one of the following device pairs:

- Basic Pedometer vs. Fitness Tracker
- Digital Camera vs. Ring Camera
- Home Stereo System vs. Smart Speaker (Alexa)

For **each** device in your pair, record:

1. What types of data does this device collect? How might it be used?
2. How might the device transmit data, if at all?
3. What privacy risks might this device introduce?

Then answer: with internet connectivity, how might a device's functionality change?

### Design a Smart Classroom
With a partner, brainstorm an IoT device for a connected classroom in a futuristic smart school. Consider:

- What is a problem or issue you'd like to address in your school?
- What kind of technology do you think we'll have by 2050?

If you're stuck, try designing a device that makes students feel safer or one that reduces distractions.

Sketch a quick design of your device, choosing from:

- **Furniture**: a desk, bookshelf, or cabinet
- **Technology**: a laptop, tablet, smart board, projector, speaker, or sensor
- **Communication**: Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, NFC, cellular, or satellite

Then describe:

- **Task Automated**: the problem your device solves and the task it performs.
- **Data Collected**: what data your device collects and how it's used.
- **Data Transmission**: how your device sends and receives that data.
- **Privacy/Security Implications**: what privacy or security concerns your device might raise.

### Give One, Get One
Share your smart classroom idea with a classmate, then listen to theirs. Record your classmate's name, their device, the data it collects, how it transmits that data, and any privacy or security concerns it raises.
