## Lesson Overview
### How did it feel when technology let you down?
Think of a time technology didn't meet your needs. In one word, how did it make you feel?

{{< collapse summary="Click here to reveal the answer." >}}

Maybe frustrated, confused, or left out. **Those feelings are signals that something isn't working for the user.**

That's what happens when designers don't start with a real person. Your job is to design something that makes users feel confident and supported instead.

{{</ collapse >}}

### Where do designers start?
**With a person and a problem, not features.** Before you design any screens, answer three questions:

1. **Who is this for?**
2. **What do they struggle with?**
3. **What do they need?**

This is [human-centered design](/glossary/human-centered-design/): understand the person, define their need, *then* design a solution. Skip it, and you'll likely build something confusing that only works for users like yourself.

### How do you build a persona?
A persona is a [user profile](/glossary/user-profile/) for one specific, realistic person. Specific details lead to better design decisions later.

Here's a persona built for this scenario: *design a better way for students to keep track of school club meetings.*

1. **Who are they?** Jordan, 10th grade, in robotics and student council.
2. **What are they going through?**
    - **Says**: "Wait, is that meeting today or tomorrow?"
    - **Thinks**: "I can't keep track of all these different group chats."
    - **Feels**: Overwhelmed, afraid of missing something
    - **Does**: Screenshots messages, checks multiple apps

3. **What's one frustration and one goal?**
    - **Frustration**: Meeting times are scattered across different platforms.
    - **Goal**: Wants one reliable place to see what's happening and when.

Be specific. Not "a student who is busy" or "too many meetings," but "missing important information because it's scattered."

### What is a problem statement?
{{% define "Problem Statement" %}}

Combine your persona's frustration and goal into one sentence:

> **[User]** needs a way to **[need]** because **[reason]**.

Your problem statement guides your later layout and navigation decisions. For example:

- If your user feels **overwhelmed**, design something simple and uncluttered.
- If your user struggles with **scattered information**, put it all on one clear dashboard.

### Which problem statement is stronger?
1. Jordan needs a way to see all club meeting updates in one place because juggling multiple group chats makes them miss important information.
2. Jordan needs an app that sends notifications.

{{< collapse summary="Click here to reveal the answer." >}}

**#1 is stronger.** #2 jumps to a solution ("an app that sends notifications") and never explains Jordan's need or why it matters.

A strong problem statement passes this checklist:

- The persona feels realistic and detailed.
- It focuses on a need, not a solution (no apps, features, or tools yet).
- It connects directly to the persona's frustration or goal.
- The "because" clause clearly explains why the need matters.

{{</ collapse >}}

### What is decomposition?
{{% define "Decomposition" %}}

Look at what you just did: you started with a messy, real person and broke it down into one specific problem you can design for. Developers use the same approach to plan code, and AI uses it to understand a structure before generating output.

### Draw Your Persona (~15mins)
Draw the persona you built in Code.org (Level 1) and get feedback on your problem statement:

1. Sketch your user, then label their name, grade, and one detail each for Says, Thinks, Feels, and Does.
2. Write their frustration, goal, and problem statement, then circle the need and underline the reason.
3. Trade with a partner and check each other's problem statements: Is it a need, not a solution? Does it connect to the frustration or goal? Does "because" explain why it matters? Then write the one strength and one suggestion your partner gave you.
