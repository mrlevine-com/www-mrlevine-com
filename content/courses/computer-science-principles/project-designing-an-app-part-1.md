## Lesson Overview
### Why is it important to consider your users when designing an app's user interface?
{{< collapse summary="Click here to reveal the answer." >}}

Developers design user interfaces to meet the needs of their users, but they don't always get it right. An interface might give too much guidance in some places and not enough in others, or it might make assumptions about its users' language or interests that turn out to be inaccurate.

Every developer brings their own experiences and perspective to a design, which makes it easy for bias to creep in without anyone noticing. Working with a partner helps you catch this bias, since someone with different experiences can point out assumptions you didn't realize you were making. Keeping an eye out for bias and collaborating with your partner throughout this project will help you design an app that truly meets your users' needs.

{{</ collapse >}}

### Why is it important to plan out the design of an app before you build it?
{{< collapse summary="Click here to reveal the answer." >}}

Planning saves you time, because it's much faster to catch problems with your user interface on paper than after you've already programmed it. Planning also gives you a chance to talk through and test your ideas with the people who will use your app, so you can make changes before you ever start coding.

The design you sketch out is called a **prototype**. A prototype helps you plan how your app will look and what features it will have before you build it in App Lab.

{{</ collapse >}}

## Assignment
{{% instructions-unit-journal-update %}}

For this project, you will work with a partner to design and build an app that teaches your classmates about a topic you both find interesting.

Your app's user interface must:

- Use at least three screens.
- Include examples of images, audio, and text.
- Be clear and easy to navigate.
- Clearly communicate information about your topic.

### Step 1: Brainstorm Topic Ideas (Day 1)
Your app can teach your classmates about any topic you and your partner agree on. Your topic could be a hobby, something you've always been interested in, a piece of your personal history, or just something you think your classmates should learn more about.

Individually brainstorm three topic ideas, then record all three.

### Step 2: Choose One Topic (Day 1)
Talk through your ideas with your partner, then together pick one topic you're both interested in teaching your classmates about.

Record your topic, explained in a few sentences describing what your app will cover. For example, if your topic were basketball, you might explain that you'll cover the rules of the game and the origin of the sport.

As you choose your topic, keep an eye out for bias, and lean on your partner to help you catch it. For example, imagine a team member wants to write "all teenagers work after-school jobs to earn spending money," because that's their own experience. Their partner, who doesn't have an after-school job, can point out that this statement doesn't hold true for every teenager.

### Step 3: Survey Your Classmates (Day 1)
To design your app, you need to understand your users. For this project, your users are your classmates.

Find two classmates and talk to them about your topic for a couple of minutes. Then record what you learned:

### Step 4: Design the User Interface (Day 1)
Based on your research, you identified **requirements** for what your app must teach your classmates. Now, sketch a **specification** that shows how your app will actually run to meet those requirements.

Include all the buttons, text, and images that a user will be able to use, and write notes or draw arrows to show how different elements are connected. For example, if clicking a button takes the user to another screen, draw an arrow from that button to a drawing of that screen.

Sketch as many screens as your app needs.

### Step 5: Create Your User Interface (Day 2)
A prototype helps you plan the look and features of your app before you program it.

**Level 1**: Look at the Bird Quiz app you explored in a previous lesson, along with the sketches that were used to design it. Run the app and compare its screens to the original design, noting how the prototype helped guide the app's development.

**Level 2**: Look at the user interface design in your Unit Journal and recreate your screens in App Lab's Design Mode.

If you and your partner divided the screens between yourselves, combine them into one project once you're both finished:

1. Choose one partner to host the finished project (**Partner A**).

3. **Partner B** shares their project's link, and **Partner A** pastes it into the import box.
4. **Partner A** selects all of the screens and assets to import.
5. **Partner A** sets the home screen to be the default screen by switching to Design Mode and clicking on the screen.
